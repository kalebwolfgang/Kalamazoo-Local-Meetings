Strategic Summary
The Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan reimagines a historically overlooked river as a vibrant
ecological and community asset. Once seen as a liability due to contamination and industrial
degradation, the Kalamazoo River is now recognized for its potential to unite neighborhoods, restore
biodiversity, and serve as a model for urban environmental renewal.

This plan is grounded in years of public input, technical analysis, and multi-agency collaboration. It maps
out a path forward for improving habitat quality, increasing equitable access to the river, and addressing
legacy environmental issues. Through a phased process involving stakeholder meetings, a public survey,
and direct engagement with communities along the corridor, six core goals were developed—ranging
from habitat restoration and recreation to education, stormwater management, and site redevelopment.

The plan identifies over two dozen projects that are aligned with these goals. These projects vary in scale
and type—from river cleanups and green infrastructure improvements to accessible kayak launches and
cultural interpretation. Together, they form an interconnected vision that restores ecological function
while honoring the corridor’s complex history.

With leadership from the City of Parchment and partners across the region, this plan reflects a shared
desire to transform the Kalamazoo River from a barrier into a bridge—linking environmental health with
community vitality. By combining science, local knowledge, and long-term vision, the Kalamazoo River
Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan offers a practical and inspiring roadmap toward a more resilient, inclusive,
and healthy future.

Plan Purpose
The Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan is rooted in the need to address the long-standing
disconnection between the community and the river—a disconnection largely caused by decades of
pollution and degradation. For much of the 20th century, the river was seen as a burden rather than a
benefit, leading to physical and psychological barriers that limited public access, enjoyment, and care.
This plan seeks to reverse that narrative by transforming the river into a vital and vibrant space for both
people and wildlife, helping to restore a sense of place and stewardship.

Reconnecting the community with the Kalamazoo River begins with meaningful engagement. By
involving residents, stakeholders, and local organizations in the planning and implementation process,
the plan not only gains broader support but also becomes more responsive to the community’s needs
and values. Active engagement fosters a shared vision and builds lasting partnerships, laying the
groundwork for sustained environmental and social investment.

With greater engagement comes renewed interest in the river, which opens the door to public education
and environmental stewardship. As people begin to see the river as a valuable natural asset rather than a
polluted afterthought, opportunities for learning, volunteerism, and conservation expand. Whether
through habitat restoration, recreational use, or community events, this plan aims to turn interest into
action, encouraging a long-term culture of stewardship and pride in the Kalamazoo River corridor.

Urban Wildlife Corridor Project Background
The idea of Urban Wildlife Corridor has roots in a 2018 Kalamazoo Community Foundation grant entitled
“Envisioning our River.” In 2018, Parchment was in the midst of writing a new community master plan
and parks and recreation plan. As community assets were articulated, it became obvious that the
Kalamazoo River is a very important asset and the city’s nearly mile long riverfront was completely
lacking from consideration. Like other mill towns, Parchment had a deeply embedded propensity to look
away from the river not towards it. Folks working on plans for a sustainable future for the community
had a different perspective and two thoughts emerged from their discussions:

    •   How might the city make a positive use of this natural asset that many older community
        members remember as a polluted liability best ignored?
    •   That question was quickly followed by the recognition that when thinking of the river and the
        connection it provides to neighbors, it was realized that it could be an asset for all. The question
        then transformed into how to bring river stakeholders together to envision river possibilities?

In 2018, the City of Parchment applied for a Kalamazoo Community Foundation grant to bring river
stakeholders and community members together. Stakeholders included regional government officials,
regional park officials, environmental and neighborhood organizations, and several recreational groups.
The stakeholders met in March 2019 for a day-long discussion to consider the environmental,
recreational, economic and educational potential of the Kalamazoo River from the City of Kalamazoo to
D Avenue in Cooper Township. Several weeks after the stakeholder session, Parchment community
members met at the Parchment Community Library to share their thoughts on the river. The group of
stakeholders had several additional meetings following that initial gathering. Many thoughtful ideas
emerged from those discussions.

In 2020, as a result of the Envisioning Our River project, The City of Parchment applied for two grants
from the Natural Resources Trustee Council for the Kalamazoo River, a coalition of state and federal
environmental agencies charged with dispensing funds from a PCB contamination settlement. The funds
were to be used to restore river ecology and enhance public use and enjoyment of the river, particularly
in communities impacted by historic PCB contamination. In January 2021, both grants were of interest to
the Trustees and were eligible for funding.

The first grant entitled, “Commerce Lane/Railroad Trestle Restoration Project,” mainly impacted
Parchment’s riverfront along Commerce Lane. Among other things, it involved the removal of an unused
railroad trestle that gathered debris and impeded river flow, and the improvement of the riverbank along
Commerce Lane in an environmentally sound way. The project was completed in 2024 and included an
accessible kayak launch and a viewing/fishing platform.

The second grant, although submitted by the City of Parchment, was written on behalf of all River
stakeholders. It recognized that the Kalamazoo River was essentially a “wild river” even though it flowed
through densely populated areas. The grant sought to identify an urban wildlife corridor along the
Kalamazoo River and its floodplain with the hope of enhancing environmental health and human
awareness of the area. This grant involves a process, including the need to:

    •   Identify and map the extent of the proposed corridor, including the river floodplain and some
        upland areas.
    •   Inventory current populations in the designated areas.
    •   Identify “pockets of recovery” to focus on the restoration of areas of environmental concern,
        value, or interest. Eventually undertake the restoration of these “pockets.”
    •   Once the corridor is delineated and areas of concern identified, develop a sort of “public
        overlay” that includes all the stakeholders. This will involve a coordinated effort to develop
        recreational and educational opportunities along the river and to make folks in the area and
        region aware of what is in their midst.
    •   The “public overlay” might include varied methods to connect people to the corridor such as
        kayak launches, delineated river trails, wildlife viewing areas, accessible birding trails, citizen
        science projects, pollinator pathways etc.

In late fall of 2021, through 2024, the Southwest Michigan Planning Commission began the process of
documenting the region, mapping the river and its environments, and giving a background of what this
essentially wild river offers the surrounding communities.

Goals and Projects
The goals and projects outlined in this section reflect the vision that emerged from the public
engagement process. Through community surveys, stakeholder meetings, and collaboration with local,
regional, and state partners, six primary goals were identified to guide the implementation of the
Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan. These goals are grounded in a shared commitment to
improving ecological health, enhancing public access, and fostering a resilient river corridor that
supports both people and wildlife.

Each goal responds to specific challenges and opportunities along the river corridor—from restoring
degraded habitat and protecting existing natural areas to expanding recreational access and addressing
legacy contamination. Together, these goals provide a framework for prioritizing actions that balance
environmental, social, and cultural values while promoting long-term stewardship.

    1. Improve habitats through restoration, invasive species management, trash cleanups and
       collaboration of local, state and federal partners.

Restoring habitat is a critical step in the ecological recovery of the Kalamazoo River Corridor. Decades of
industrial activity, development, and pollution have disrupted natural systems and diminished native
ecosystems. Focused efforts like invasive species management, native plantings, wetland restoration,
and trash removal help to reestablish ecological function, improve biodiversity, and build resilience to
climate-related impacts. These visible improvements not only benefit the environment but also
strengthen community connections to the river by signaling progress and renewal.

This goal is one of the most active components of the plan, with 18 projects identified. Many focus on
large-scale ecological restoration such as stream daylighting and habitat connectivity, while others aim
to reduce pollution and improve wildlife movement through initiatives like in-stream trash capture
systems. These projects rely on strong partnerships among local governments, nonprofit organizations,
and tribal nations, highlighting the collaborative approach necessary to restore and sustain the health of
the corridor.

    2. Protect and preserve existing areas of high-quality habitat.

While restoring damaged areas is vital, protecting high-quality habitat that already supports healthy
ecosystems is just as important. Riparian forests, wetlands, and nesting areas provide essential services
like water filtration, flood mitigation, and wildlife refuge. Preserving these areas safeguards the
ecological value that still exists within the corridor and helps prevent further fragmentation. It also
supports broader conservation efforts by maintaining connectivity and reinforcing the natural systems
that many species rely on.

Three projects have been identified under this goal, including the protection of a heron rookery near
Glenn Allen Island and the evaluation of floodplain parcels that may not be suitable for development but
hold high conservation potential. To guide future efforts, the plan recommends a formal needs
assessment to identify and prioritize additional areas for preservation. The Multi-Criteria Decision
Analysis (MCDA), detailed in the Potential Preservation Areas section, provides a strong foundation for
this work—highlighting over 4,700 acres of habitat that may harbor significant ecological value.
Together, these tools and strategies offer a proactive approach to conservation that prioritizes long-
term resilience and ecological integrity.

    3. Enhance recreational assets and access along the river corridor while safeguarding existing
        high-quality resources.
Providing access to the Kalamazoo River for recreation such as kayaking, walking, biking, and
birdwatching, helps build public support for its protection. Safe, equitable access to nature improves
physical and mental health, fosters community connection, and drives local economic activity. Enhancing
recreational opportunities along the river must be done with care to protect sensitive natural areas, but
when balanced well, it promotes both human well-being and environmental stewardship.

This goal includes 16 proposed projects aimed at strengthening public interaction with the river. These
range from new kayak launches and improved trail connections to enhanced picnic areas and fishing
spots. Several projects also prioritize universal design, adding features like accessible boardwalks and
seating to ensure people of all ages and abilities can enjoy the river. The emphasis on low-impact,
inclusive recreation reflects the plan’s broader commitment to equity, environmental stewardship, and
community vitality.

    4. Provide opportunities for education – passive and active interpretation and use of different
       media to ensure the success of projects, safety of visitors and to further share information of
       ecological, historical and cultural significance.

Education is a powerful tool for shaping how communities understand and care for the Kalamazoo River
corridor. By increasing awareness of the river’s ecological importance and its complex cultural and
historical context including the impacts of industrial pollution and the legacy of Indigenous stewardship,
educational initiatives help foster a deeper sense of connection and shared responsibility. Interpretive
signage, public art, and hands-on learning opportunities can inspire long-term engagement and promote
informed, community-driven stewardship.

While five projects are dedicated specifically to education, many others incorporate interpretive
elements as part of broader goals. These include river-focused art installations, interactive signage with
QR codes linking to historical and ecological content, and educational programming embedded within
restoration projects. Education functions as a key thread throughout the plan to enhance public
understanding, improve visitor safety, and reinforce the long-term success of both environmental and
recreational efforts.

    5. Implement best practices to mitigate stormwater, sediment, and nutrient issues to improve
       habitat and water quality.

Managing stormwater is essential to protecting both the health of the Kalamazoo River and the
surrounding communities. In urban areas, runoff often carries sediment, nutrients, and pollutants
directly into waterways, contributing to erosion, degraded habitats, and water quality concerns. Green
infrastructure and best practices like rain gardens, wetland restoration, and stream daylighting, not only
reduce flooding but also filter pollutants and support resilient, functioning ecosystems. These strategies
are increasingly important as communities adapt to the impacts of climate change and work to
safeguard public and environmental health.

Although only two projects in the plan focus specifically on stormwater mitigation, many others
incorporate stormwater-related improvements through habitat restoration, floodplain enhancement,
and the use of natural infrastructure. These projects help manage runoff at the source and reduce its

impact downstream. Looking ahead, future implementation phases will continue to emphasize low-
impact development and integrated stormwater solutions as essential components of a healthy,

    6. Implement redevelopment projects on blighted or contaminated sites with a focus on
       restoring habitat, increasing access to the river or natural systems and maintaining community
       character.

Sections of the Kalamazoo River corridor continue to bear the scars of past industrial activity, with
contaminated, vacant, or underutilized sites scattered throughout the landscape. These areas, while
challenged by legacy pollution, offer significant potential for transformation. Thoughtful redevelopment
that incorporates environmental restoration, public access, and cultural recognition can turn these
brownfields into valued community assets—helping to repair environmental damage while supporting
neighborhood revitalization and equitable access to the river.

While this goal includes one primary redevelopment project, several other efforts involve evaluating
sites with known or suspected contamination for possible reuse or restoration. These projects not only
address longstanding environmental issues but also create space for new trails, gathering areas, or
restored habitat. Alignment with existing master plans and environmental cleanup strategies will be
essential for successful implementation.

Projects
To move from planning to action, a series of potential projects have been identified that align with the
six overarching goals. These projects reflect a range of strategies, from on-the-ground restoration and
green infrastructure to education, interpretation, and access improvements. Many of the ideas
presented here stem directly from community input and are designed to be flexible, scalable, and
responsive to local needs.

The following table organizes specific projects under each goal to demonstrate how proposed actions
can support and reinforce the broader vision of the corridor. These projects are intended to serve as a
starting point for collaboration, investment, and phased implementation as resources and partnerships
develop over time.

Potential Restoration and Preservation Areas
One goal of this plan is to identify high-quality areas for potential restoration or preservation. within the
corridor where restoration and preservation efforts can make the greatest impact. These areas within
the corridor may represent environmental concern, value, or interest. To define and evaluate these
locations, a GIS-based analysis was conducted that brings together multiple natural features into a clear,
visual framework for decision-making.

In sections of this plan, various maps will illustrate the natural assets within the corridor. The Wetlands
map highlights areas along rivers and streams, while the Land Cover/Land Use maps depict the
distribution of forests, fields, and water. However, each map presents individual features without
synthesizing the natural landscape into a holistic interpretation. The key question being addressed is:
where are the best locations to prioritize preservation and restoration projects?

To integrate natural assets, a GIS analysis was conducted in the corridor using a method called "Multi-
Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) and GIS." This approach combines geographic information systems
(GIS) with MCDA techniques, enabling decision-makers to evaluate spatial data across multiple criteria.
By visualizing various factors related to natural resources on a map, this process highlights the best
opportunities to restore or preserve areas of high environmental value across the corridor.

The analysis begins by identifying key spatial features that are critical for guiding preservation efforts. To
highlight the importance of certain features, spatial buffers were applied, thereby assigning them greater
weight in the decision-making process. Each feature and its corresponding buffer were scored based on
their preservation value.

preservation. This step ensured that the final output remained focused, relevant, and strategically
targeted.

Results

The MCDA analysis produced scores ranging from 0 to 160, which were categorized into four groups
using a quantile distribution, with zero values excluded. A total of 4,767 acres, representing 31% of the
corridor, received a score, while the remaining 69% was excluded due to land use types that were not
considered relevant to preservation efforts or were not identified in the criteria analysis.

Other high-scoring areas are found along Davis Creek, with particularly dense concentrations along

Limitations of this analysis include reliance on satellite-derived data, the overall accuracy of the datasets,
and the potential for logical assumptions that could lead to misleading results. Processed satellite
imagery was used to identify natural habitats and exclude developed regions from consideration. While
satellite imagery generally maintains an accuracy of 80%-90%, many low-density residential areas
remained in the analysis due to the difficulty of detecting less densely built environments, particularly
those with significant tree cover or forested landscapes. Additionally, river and stream datasets may have
inaccuracies of up to 40 feet, leading to similar discrepancies in the buffer calculations. As a result, the
derived layer of Potential Restoration and Preservation Areas may not always accurately reflect areas
suitable for preservation due to existing on-the-ground conditions. However, this layer does highlight
valuable natural resources that warrant further investigation for potential preservation and restoration.

Planning Process
The Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan was developed through a two-phase process that
prioritized collaboration, transparency, and community input. From the beginning, the planning process
was designed to bring together a wide range of voices including residents, community leaders,
government agencies, and local organizations. Their opinions help shape a shared vision for the future of
the river corridor. Over the course of more than two years, this inclusive effort moved from foundational
research and stakeholder engagement to plan development, public outreach, and refinement of goals
and projects based on broad community feedback.

Timeline for Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor

Phase I – Background Information & Stakeholder Engagement
The planning process officially began with a kickoff meeting on December 16, 2022, where the Steering
Committee convened to launch the master plan and feasibility study. A follow-up meeting on March 15,
2023, focused on updates to the process and preparation for broader stakeholder engagement.

On June 23, 2023, the first stakeholder meeting was held, bringing together 18 stakeholders to discuss
key opportunities, challenges, ongoing projects, and data needs within the corridor. These early
conversations helped shape the initial direction of the plan and are summarized in Appendix C.

Phase II – Plan Development
In March 2024, Phase II began with a Steering Committee meeting focused on refining the planning
approach. This was followed by a June 21, 2024 meeting, where the committee reviewed the draft public
survey, discussed project timelines, and provided input on early plan goals and objectives.

A public survey ran from June 25 to September 20, 2024, and was distributed widely through social
media, municipal websites, utility bills, and a press release. A total of 113 respondents participated,
sharing feedback on restoration priorities, recreational access, and educational opportunities. High-
priority issues included soil contamination, habitat preservation, invasive species management,
stormwater improvements, and river access. Respondents also expressed strong interest in learning
about the corridor’s cultural and environmental history, especially Native American heritage and the
legacy of industrial pollution. Full survey results can be found in Appendix B.

Existing Conditions
The Kalamazoo River is located entirely within the state of Michigan and is the seventh largest
river in Michigan, draining over 2,000 square miles and spanning over eight counties. The South
Branch of the Kalamazoo River begins in Hillsdale County, and the North Branch begins in
Jackson County. The branches meet in Calhoun County and continue flowing through Kalamazoo
County and Allegan County, connecting over nineteen communities before reaching Lake
Michigan at the City of Saugatuck. The study area for the Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife
Corridor is focused on the area along the Kalamazoo River, from the Morrow Dam to D Avenue
within Kalamazoo County.

Waterways
The Kalamazoo River Corridor is rich in hydrological diversity, encompassing 11.8 miles of the Kalamazoo
River, 41.6 miles of streams, and 12.75 miles of county drains. This network of waterways includes
tributaries of the Kalamazoo River such as Portage Creek, Spring Brook, Spring Valley Creek, and various
county drains. Together, these features provide habitat for a variety of aquatic and riparian species. The
corridor also contains over 158 acres of lakes, most of which were created by the construction of eleven
dams throughout the region. Notable lakes include Spring Valley Lake and portions of Morrow Lake,
which serve as important ecological and recreational resources.

Within the City of Kalamazoo, the river is generally 50 to 150 feet wide, though some sections may be
narrower or wider based on natural features and past industrial modifications. While the river runs
through an urban area, it retains a natural feel in many sections, with parks and green spaces providing a
buffer from the city.

Spring Brook is a small second-order tributary to the Kalamazoo River, located in Cooper Township and
Richland Township. It is a high-quality designated trout stream with a top-quality coldwater designation.
Spring Brook enters the Kalamazoo River 5 miles north of downtown Kalamazoo (MDNR 1992).

These water features play a vital role in shaping the
health and connectivity of the corridor. The Kalamazoo
River and its tributaries create natural pathways, while
the lakes provide habitats for fish, amphibians,
waterfowl, and other species. The presence of dams
has also altered the natural flow patterns, posing
challenges for wildlife movement. Restoration efforts,
such as dam removals and wetland rehabilitation, offer
opportunities to reconnect these waterways and
enhance the corridor’s ecological function.

Topography
The Corridor is characterized by a dynamic landscape of low-lying floodplains and steep elevation
changes shaped over thousands of years by glacial activity and river processes (Map 4). As the glacier
receded, it deposited a mix of sediment and rocks, creating moraines that run along either side of the
corridor. These moraines can be seen by the rapid changes in elevation in Map 4. Elevations within the
corridor range significantly, creating a variety of microhabitats that support diverse plant and animal
species, all of which play a crucial role in the corridor’s overall biodiversity.

Looking at the topography in the Corridor starting in the north, in Cooper Township, the Kalamazoo River
meanders through low-lying floodplains, where the land is relatively flat and prone to seasonal flooding.
These areas support wetlands, marshes, and riparian forests. Parchment sits slightly higher in elevation
than Kalamazoo, with steeper bluffs near the river. The riverbanks here are more defined, with some
wooded slopes and small ravines leading down to the water. In the City of Kalamazoo, the river flows
through an urban environment, but much of its corridor is lined with forested floodplains and wetlands.
In Comstock Township the topography becomes more varied, with wider floodplains and wetlands along
the river. The land is a mix of low-lying areas prone to seasonal flooding and higher, rolling hills further
from the river.

Municipalities
The Kalamazoo River Corridor spans six municipalities, covering a total of 15,500 acres and varying
significantly in its share of each municipality's land area (Map 5). The City of Kalamazoo contains the
largest portion of the corridor, with 5,627 acres, accounting for 35.2% of its municipal area. As the most
urbanized part of the corridor, this area presents both challenges and opportunities for conservation.
Urban parks, riparian buffers, and green infrastructure projects in the City of Kalamazoo can play a
pivotal role in maintaining connectivity and enhancing habitat within this highly developed landscape.
Similarly, Kalamazoo Township contributes 2,497 acres, representing 32.9% of its land area, offering a
mix of suburban and natural environments that are critical to the corridor's ecological health.

The City of Parchment, though smaller in total size, has the highest percentage of its land included in the
corridor, with 62.7% of its municipal area—376 acres—falling within the boundary. This underscores the

Human Impacts
The impact humans have on the Kalamazoo River is evident and far-reaching. From extensive urban
development along its banks to historical pollution from industrial waste and chemical dumping, the
river has been altered in ways that will persist for generations. Efforts have been made to restore and
protect the river, but the legacy of contamination and habitat degradation continues to shape its
ecological health.

Impervious Surfaces and Habitat Fragmentation

As the Kalamazoo River Corridor has grown, the expansion of roads, parking lots, and buildings has
increased impervious surface coverage, leading to stormwater runoff that carries pollutants directly into
the Kalamazoo River and its tributaries. This runoff may contain oil, heavy metals, and sediment, which
degrade water quality and disrupt aquatic habitats. Implementing green infrastructure solutions, such as
rain gardens, bioswales, and expanded riparian buffers, can help filter pollutants before they reach
critical water sources. Additionally, urbanization has fragmented critical wildlife corridors, making it
difficult for species to move between green spaces. Animals such as deer, foxes, and migratory birds
struggle to navigate an increasingly urbanized environment, leading to higher instances of human-
wildlife interactions, including wildlife-vehicle incidents.

Pollution Sites and Environmental Contamination

The Kalamazoo River has long been impacted by industrial pollution, particularly from paper mills that
historically dumped PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls) into the waterway. These toxic chemicals persist in
the river’s sediment, impacting fish populations and posing risks to human health. Portions of the river
remain designated as a Superfund site, requiring long-term remediation efforts to remove contamination
and restore ecological function. In addition to legacy pollution, waste disposal, illegal dumping, and
agricultural runoff introduce pesticides, fertilizers, and microplastics into the water, further straining the
river’s ability to support healthy aquatic life.

Wellhead Protection Areas and Water Quality

The Kalamazoo River Corridor plays a crucial role in groundwater recharge and drinking water supply for
many communities in Kalamazoo County. Wellhead protection areas, designed to safeguard these water
sources, are increasingly threatened by urban runoff, industrial contaminants, and stormwater pollution.
Without proper land-use planning and conservation efforts, there is a risk of contamination spreading
into groundwater, affecting both human populations and the broader ecosystem.

By acknowledging these human impacts, the integration of sustainable development, habitat restoration,
and pollution mitigation strategies can be implemented to protect the river for future generations.
Reducing impervious surfaces, remediating contaminated sites, and expanding protected areas will be
essential steps toward restoring the ecological integrity of the river while ensuring it remains a valuable
natural resource for both wildlife and the people of the region.

Impervious Surfaces
Impervious surfaces are defined as areas which prevent or impede the ability for stormwater to infiltrate into the soil. Common impervious
surfaces include streets, parking lots, roofs, patios, driveways, compacted gravel surfaces, and fabric or plastic coverings. In undeveloped areas,
soil and plants absorb and filter rainfall, which slows down the movement of water, and allows time for it to seep into the soil and recharge
groundwater. However, in areas with impervious surfaces like parking lots, roofs, and streets, rainwater flows across the surface. This movement
across unimpeded surfaces increases the water’s capacity to carry with it any sediments, contaminants, trash, and other pollutants. The runoff is
typically collected into pipes and eventually discharged into the Kalamazoo River or the water treatment plant. In cases of heavy rain, treatment
plants may become overwhelmed and discharge the flow directly into the river. This has further implications for downstream flooding as more
water is piped into the river and carried to areas of lower elevation. Moreover, the water quality of the river fluctuates with each rainstorm as
pollutants collected by stormwater are carried into the river’s waters.

Within the Kalamazoo River Corridor, 50% of the land is covered by 0-10% of impervious surface, allowing for ample infiltration and filtration of
stormwater (Map 10). However, 22% of the corridor is covered in 61-100% impervious surface, mostly located within the Cities of Parchment and
Kalamazoo. From 1975 to 2016, there was a 24% increase of impervious surface in the corridor.

This represents an opportunity for areas with high impervious surfaces to implement green infrastructure. Green infrastructure refers to living
infrastructure that mimics ecological systems, and includes bioswales, rain gardens, and constructed wetlands. Green infrastructure allows
stormwater to infiltrate natural systems gradually after storm events, which lessens flooding, erosion, and pollution. When green infrastructure
utilizes vegetation, it can purify excess water and lessen pollution from farms, city streets, lawns, and vehicles. Green infrastructure could be
used in parks, along roads, or in areas with standing water after storm events.

Pollution Sites within the Corridor
Map 11 shows locations of brownfields, superfund sites, PFAs sites, and leaking underground storage
tanks (LUSTs) throughout the corridor. Brownfields are developed sites that are not presently being
utilized to their full extent due to historical contamination or perceived environmental conditions. These
sites may include former gas stations, refineries, manufacturing plants, railroads, paper mills, or other
past land uses that may have used heavy chemicals or have underground storage tanks. Superfund sites
are locations where hazardous waste was historically dumped and landfills. PFAS sites are sources of
PFAS contamination. They are where the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy
(EGLE) has a valid groundwater monitoring well sample result that exceeds one or more of Michigan’s
seven PFAS groundwater cleanup criteria and have identified this location as the source of PFAS
contamination. These sites may include fire-training areas, landfills, and paper manufacturers. LUSTs are
sites with underground storage tanks that leak toxic materials, including gasoline and waste oil.

Within the corridor, there are two superfund sites, Allied Paper, Inc./Portage Creek/Kalamazoo River, and
Auto Ion Chemicals, Inc. The Allied Paper, Inc./Portage Creek/Kalamazoo River includes an Allied Paper,
Inc. property in the Corridor, a 3-mile stretch of the Portage Creek to where the creek meets the
Kalamazoo River, and an 80-mile stretch of the Kalamazoo River. The stretch of the Kalamazoo River
designated as a Superfund site begins at Morrow Dam, goes through the Urban Wildlife Corridor, and
follows the river through Saugatuck to Lake Michigan. This site was listed as a superfund site in 1990 due
to the presence of Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs), a human-caused forever chemical that does not
break down in the environment overtime. At this superfund site, PCBs were used in the manufacturing of
carbonless copy paper from 1957 to 1971. In 1977, the Michigan Department of Public Health issued an
advisory against eating fish from the Kalamazoo River due to the presence of PCBs, and after a multi-year
study conducted by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources (MDNR), PCBs were found in the
sediment, fish, and water column of the Kalamazoo River and Portage Creek. Moreover, the study found
that about 142,000 people were drinking water from wells that were also impacted by PCB
contamination. Clean up of this superfund site began in 1998 and is expected to be completed in 2026.

The second superfund site in the corridor, Auto Ion Chemicals, Inc., is a 1-acre area that was listed as a
superfund in 1983. This site was operated by a company that treated chromium plating waste, and
during the operation had numerous discharges to the ground, sewers, and Kalamazoo River. These
discharges included hexavalent chromium and cyanide. This site was cleaned up in 1994 and included
the removal of about 122,000 gallons of liquid plating waste and sludges in basements and an outside
lagoon.

Wellhead Protection Areas
The Michigan Wellhead Protection Program aims to safeguard the drinking water supply of the state's
communities by minimizing groundwater contamination risks and averting costly clean-up efforts. This
program identifies and protects Wellhead Protection Areas (WHPAs) surrounding water wells or well
fields, where contaminants could potentially migrate and affect water quality. Type 1 Provisional
Wellhead Protection Areas are areas meant to protect community public water supplies, while Type 2
Provisional Wellhead Areas are areas meant to protect water systems that regularly serve at least 25
people but are not considered community water systems. Traditional Wellhead Protection and
Delineation areas are identified via ground water supply studies in and around the well or well field.

Within the corridor, there are over 2,580 acres in various wellhead protection areas, with the majority
located in Traditional Wellhead Protection areas (Map 12). Traditional Wellhead Protection areas make
up a total of 2,541.7 acres of the corridor, and are concentrated around Axtell Creek, Portage Creek,
Spring Valley Creek, and Schippers Lane Drain. A traditional wellhead protection area also exists north of
the City of Parchment within Cooper Township. The largest section of the type 1 wellhead protection
area within the corridor is a 3.3 acre area located in the north/northwest corner, in Cooper Township,
along a tributary stream to the Kalamazoo River. The type 2 wellhead protection area is also in Cooper
Township, 38.8 acre.

Some best Management Practices for Agriculture & Industry in Traditional Wellhead Protection areas are
to promote buffer zones around wellheads to reduce pesticide and fertilizer runoff. Also to encourage
the use of low-impact development techniques to manage stormwater and minimize pollution. Public
awareness programs and providing training for local businesses, farmers and residents on pollution
prevention practices are important activities to protect a safe water supply.

Habitat Quality
The habitat cores and fragments within the corridor are unevenly distributed, with the majority of intact
habitat cores—primarily of medium and low quality—located in the northern section (Map 14). In
contrast, the City of Kalamazoo and the City of Parchment contain no intact habitat cores, consisting
instead of numerous habitat fragments. These fragmented areas present challenges for wildlife
movement and biodiversity but also create opportunities to establish habitat connections throughout
the corridor. By strategically linking habitat fragments with intact cores, especially in the northern
section, a more connected and resilient ecological network can be developed.

Key natural corridors, such as river systems and green spaces, play a crucial role in supporting
biodiversity and facilitating species movement through urbanized areas. However, major roads, railroads,
and ongoing urban development pose significant fragmentation challenges. To counteract these effects,
intact habitat cores should be preserved and reinforced with well-designed habitat corridors.
Strengthening these connections will help create a more sustainable urban ecosystem, ensuring long-
term ecological resilience and improving the coexistence of urban development with natural habitats.

Floodplains
A floodplain is the low-lying land adjacent to a river or stream that naturally absorbs and disperses
excess water during periods of heavy rainfall or snowmelt, reducing the risk of downstream flooding.
Map 16 illustrates the floodplain of the Kalamazoo River Corridor, highlighting areas with a 1% annual
chance of flooding (commonly known as the 100-year floodplain). The 1% annual chance flood, often
referred to as the 100-year flood, is a term used by FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency) to
describe an area that has a 1 in 100 chance of flooding in any given year. It does not mean that the flood
will only happen once every 100 years—rather, it is a statistical probability. Over the course of a 30-year
mortgage, for example, a property in a 1% annual chance flood zone has about a 26% chance of
experiencing a flood. These areas are typically shown as Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs) on FEMA’s
Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs), and they often require flood insurance if a federally backed
mortgage is involved.

These flood hazard areas are primarily concentrated along the river and its tributaries, including Portage
Creek, Davis Creek, and Arcadia Creek. The floodplain extends through the Cities of Kalamazoo and
Parchment, as well as surrounding townships, affecting both developed and natural areas. Given that
much of the river corridor is within the floodplain, these areas play a crucial role in managing
floodwaters, supporting wetland ecosystems, and maintaining water quality. However, urban
development and infrastructure within flood-prone zones increase the risk of property damage and
environmental degradation during extreme weather events.

In 2008, the Kalamazoo River experienced severe flooding due to the remnants of hurricanes, which
brought over 10 inches of rain to Kalamazoo County. Approximately 466 homes and 10 businesses were
flooded. Ten major street intersections in downtown Kalamazoo were submerged, leading to widespread
road closures. A local State of Emergency was declared. Again in 2018 in the City of Kalamazoo and
adjacent Townships, the Kalamazoo River experienced severe flooding due to a combination of factors: a
melting snowpack and an extended period of rainfall. Hundreds of homeowners experienced property
flooding, with basements and yards inundated and numerous roads were submerged.

In 2024, the city has teamed up with National Weather Service, United States Geological Survey, and the
U.S. Army Corps of Engineers on a joint Flood Inundation Mapping (FIM) project, surveying the area of
the Kalamazoo River and Portage Creek to create a 2D model to track streamflow of their surrounding
floodplains and find ways to redirect floodwaters and mitigate the damage they do.

Preserving and restoring natural floodplain functions within the Kalamazoo River Corridor is essential for
mitigating flood risks and enhancing ecological resilience. The river corridor, highlighted in green,
overlaps significantly with flood-prone areas, making it an ideal focus for conservation and flood
management efforts. Strategies such as wetland restoration, floodplain zoning regulations, and green
infrastructure solutions can help reduce flood impacts while providing habitat for wildlife. Additionally,
urban planning should prioritize flood-resilient development, ensuring that infrastructure and residential
areas remain protected from future flood events. By integrating flood management with habitat
conservation, the region can create a more sustainable and climate-resilient landscape.

Wetlands
Wetlands serve as vital ecological assets within the Kalamazoo River Corridor, playing a crucial role in
water filtration, flood control, and wildlife habitat. Historically, Michigan had approximately 11 million
acres of wetlands, but today, only 3 million acres remain, a drastic reduction due to urban expansion and
agricultural development. Within the Kalamazoo River watershed, many wetlands have been significantly
altered by human activities, with large areas drained or filled for development.

Despite these losses, the remaining wetlands function as natural filters, reducing sedimentation and
nutrient runoff into the Kalamazoo River and its tributaries, including Portage Creek, Davis Creek, and
Spring Valley Creek. They also play a crucial role in flood prevention, absorbing excess water and
reducing flood risk in the City of Kalamazoo, City of Parchment, and surrounding townships. Additionally,
wetlands provide habitat for a diverse range of wildlife, including waterfowl, amphibians, fish, and rare
wetland species. Protecting these wetlands is essential not only for preserving biodiversity but also for
ensuring the long-term ecological health of the Kalamazoo River system.

Part 303 of NREPA seeks to protect wetland resources by regulating land that meets the statutory
definition of a wetland, based on vegetation, water table, and soil type. Certain activities require a
permit from the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) if a site meets
the wetland definition. However, some activities are exempt, including fishing, trapping, hunting, hiking,
existing farm operations, and forest product harvesting.

Wetland areas subject to EGLE regulation include wetlands, regardless of size, that are contiguous to or
within 500 feet of the ordinary high water mark of any lake, stream, or pond; wetlands larger than five
acres that are not contiguous to any lake, stream, or pond; and wetlands that are not contiguous but are
essential to preserving natural resources.

The Kalamazoo River Corridor still contains a substantial number of wetlands. Map 17 highlights both
existing wetlands (1,707 acres) and areas with potential for wetland restoration (3,452 acres). These
potential restoration areas include wet soil that may have once been wetlands but were later drained.
However, they present valuable opportunities for restoration, improving habitat connectivity and water
quality.

Map 18 highlights the habitat function of wetlands in the corridor, with regards to their role in
supporting migration, breeding grounds and general habitat for key wildlife species. These species
include waterfowl, shorebirds, interior forest birds, amphibians, fish, and rare wetland species. The
higher scored wetlands (red and orange) are particularly important for wildlife conservation, and are
located throughout the length of the corridor, but are mostly concentrated along the Kalamazoo River.
Yellow and green areas indicate wetlands with lower habitat function scores (1-4), which still contribute
to biodiversity but may be smaller, more isolated, or degraded by human activity. Existing wetlands are
mapped, along with wetlands that do not meet specific habitat criteria, highlighting areas where
restoration or protection efforts could enhance ecological value.

Regional collaboration across municipalities in the Kalamazoo River Corridor is essential for long-term
wetland preservation and restoration. By implementing green infrastructure, enforcing wetland
protection policies, and promoting sustainable land use practices, the region can enhance flood
absorption capacity, expand high-quality habitats, and create safer recreational opportunities.

Conclusions
The Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan presents a comprehensive inventory of ecological,
recreational, and municipal assets within the corridor. Through detailed analysis of environmental
features—such as wetlands, habitat cores, impervious surfaces, pollution sites, and recreational
amenities, this plan reveals a region rich in natural assets and opportunities. It identifies clear
opportunities for habitat restoration, pollution remediation, and inclusive community engagement that
collectively reframe the river as a vital regional asset.

The Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor offers a rare opportunity to reclaim and reimagine a
historically impacted landscape as a model for regional ecological restoration and community
revitalization. By engaging stakeholders, including project opportunities, and emphasizing the many
benefits of a connected green corridor, this plan lays the foundation for a more sustainable, vibrant
future.

Successful implementation will rely on sustained collaboration, clear priorities, and broad engagement.
Advancing the corridor’s goals—such as linking ecological networks, enhancing stormwater
management, and creating inclusive recreational spaces—will require coordination across jurisdictions
and alignment with local plans and ordinances. Equally important is maintaining focus on community
benefits: improved water quality, increased biodiversity, safer and more accessible public spaces, and
strengthened connections between people and nature.

To ensure momentum, it is recommended to establish a clear roadmap for near-term actions and long-
term goals. This includes leveraging the Master Plan reviews to influence zoning and ordinances,
applying for targeted funding, and supporting grassroots actions through environmental education and
community partnerships.

Public involvement must remain at the heart of the process. Sustained outreach, volunteer engagement,
and inclusive participation, particularly from youth and historically underrepresented communities, will
help ensure the corridor evolves in a way that reflects the needs and aspirations of the people it serves.

Ultimately, the Kalamazoo River Urban Wildlife Corridor Plan represents more than a series of maps and
projects. It is a call to action—to reconnect with the river, repair what has been damaged, and reclaim a
shared natural resource for future generations. With continued commitment and creativity, the corridor
can become a place of renewal, not only for the landscape but for the communities it weaves together.

Q9 Are there any specific cultural or historical aspects of the river
corridor that should be highlighted?
The survey responses suggest a strong interest in highlighting both the Native American and industrial
histories of the Kalamazoo River corridor. Many emphasized the importance of recognizing Indigenous
uses of the river, including spiritual practices, fishing, hunting, and stewardship of the land, with a call to
engage Native American voices in future projects. Additionally, respondents focused on the significant
environmental impact of the paper mills and the need to educate the public on the resulting pollution
and restoration efforts. Some respondents supported the idea of interpretive signage, while others
preferred to limit it to avoid disrupting the natural beauty of the area. Overall, there is a desire to
balance cultural recognition, historical education, and ecological preservation.

Q10 Do you have ideas for future projects in this region that would
enhance recreational opportunities for this corridor?
The survey responses provide numerous ideas for enhancing recreational opportunities along the
Kalamazoo River corridor. Many respondents suggested improving access points, particularly for kayaking
and canoeing, with calls for better signage, handicap-accessible launches, and more locations, such as in
Galesburg and Parchment. There is interest in trail development, including walking and biking paths,
connecting existing trails, and making them accessible for seniors. Safety concerns were raised regarding
homeless populations and road crossings for wildlife. River cleanups, removal of debris, and addressing
pollution were frequently mentioned as priorities. Educational opportunities, including art installations,
historical markers, and environmental education, were also highlighted, along with the preservation of
native plants and habitats. Respondents expressed a desire for natural areas to remain undisturbed
while balancing recreation and ecological restoration efforts.

Q11 Are there specific educational topics you would like to see made
available throughout the corridor?
The survey responses emphasize a wide range of educational topics that residents would like to see
highlighted along the Kalamazoo River corridor. Many suggested focusing on the river's natural history,
including native plants, animals, and habitats, with specific interest in bird species, pollinators, and
wildlife conservation. There is a desire to educate people on the history of the river, including its Native
American usage, the impact of the paper industry, and ongoing cleanup efforts. Topics such as ecosystem
health, sustainability, invasive species, and water pollution were frequently mentioned, along with
practical advice on how individuals can help preserve the environment. Respondents also suggested
interactive and experiential learning, like guided tours, birding trails, and educational signage that
connects people to the river’s ecological and historical significance. Several people expressed an interest
in learning more about the water cycle, watershed protection, and how local actions can support the
river's health.
