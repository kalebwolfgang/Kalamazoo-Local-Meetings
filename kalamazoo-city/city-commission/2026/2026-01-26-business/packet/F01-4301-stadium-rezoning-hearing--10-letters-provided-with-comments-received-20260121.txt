Dear Mayor, Vice-Mayor, Commissioners and City Manager:

We write this letter with deep concern for Our beautiful Asylum Preserve which is so
dear to us and Kalamazoo residents from all neighborhoods and beyond, who use it all

beauty as it is now and am sure that you feel the same. This environmentally sensitive

equipped to handle additional pollution run-off.

We strongly oppose the new developer’s current request to rezone 4301 Stadium Drive

       includes much more than a single Future Land Use Map in the contents of both the City’s Master Plan and

      While the rezoning application claim of consistency relies almost entirely on the Master Plan’s “Future Land Use Map”

be appropriate for the property), it is clear that any type of zoning of the property that would allow for intense commercial

Master Plan. This includes, but is not limited to, (i) the City’s focus on “Complete Neighborhoods: equity and quality of
life, invest in open space, arts and culture” (Master Plan, p. 8); and (ii) the City’s Strategic Vision goal of Environmental

    B. There are no changed conditions that “require” the subject property to be rezoned for high intensity
       commercial use such as CC zoning.

     An application to rezone the subject property to CC was previously reviewed at length by the City Planning
Commission in 2020 and the recommendation of the Planning Commission at that time was to deny the rezoning
application. Conditions have not changed significantly since 2020 so as to “require” any different analysis or result other
than the same recommendation for rezoning denial. The Planning Commission recognized the continued lack of changed
conditions in making its recent recommendation to this City Commission to deny the CC rezoning application. While the
subject parcel is now “split zoning” between single family residential (RS-5) and multi-family residential (RM-15), that is a
problem of the current property owner’s own making by choosing to combine differently zoned parcels and therefore does
not “require” rezoning the property, much less require rezoning it to CC. Simply put, the rezoning application prima facie
fails to show that “CC” zoning is “required” and also falsely claims that “many of the properties within the Stadium Drive
corridor have been developed or redeveloped for commercial uses in recent years.” The fact is that the commercial
properties along Stadium Drive in the City of Kalamazoo have been commercial properties for many decades and in any
case do not abut both a nature preserve and numerous residences. Ironically, the subject property does not directly abut
any commercial property whatsoever.

    Rather, and again relying solely on the Future Land Use Map developed by City staff rather than by community
members, the rezoning application resorts to improperly equating the Future Land Use Map to be the “community
perspective” and wrongly claims that as such, the “community perspective” is that the property is best suited for “some
form of commercial use.” The rezoning application then also fails to provide any basis for why “some form of commercial
use” “requires” CC zoning (the most intense type of commercial use in the City Zoning Code).

     It is clear that the Future Land Use Map is not the “community perspective.” It is well documented history that the
people and organizations within the City of Kalamazoo have strongly opposed prior applications to rezone the subject
property to CC. The subject property is located entirely within the Oakland Drive-Winchell Neighborhood (“ODWN”) so
any future development of the property will have a direct impact on the quality of life of ODWN residents and businesses.
The ODWNA and the residents for whom it advocates have repeatedly and consistently given City staff, the City Planning
Commission, and the City Commission overwhelming feedback that, in the actual “community’s perspective,” the subject
property is not best suited for commercial uses allowed under CC zoning and that it is definitely not “in the general public’s
interest for [this portion of] the Stadium Drive corridor to be developed for commercial purposes.” The ODWNA
recognizes that while the City’s Future Land Use Map has designated the property that is the subject of this zoning
request for “commercial” use, neither the Plan nor the Map specify or even recommend which specific commercial zoning
district(s) might be appropriate, much less which type might be “required.” There are several commercial districts allowing
for uses that are much less intense than CC. Also, as discussed below in the Michigan Court of Appeals opinion in
Tollbrook, LLC v. City of Troy, under Michigan law the contents of a master plan do not “require” any zoning
changes.

    Further, the conditions surrounding the subject property remain as they have for decades. The property lies on the

adjacent properties for intense commercial development is not the community perspective and in any case, is not
“required.”

    C. There is no demonstrated community need to rezone the subject property for intense commercial
       development; rather, the community need demonstrated by the people of the City of Kalamazoo is for
       protection of the green, open, and environmentally sensitive natural areas within the City.

     The rezoning application fails to specifically identify a single “community need” to rezone the subject property to CC,
again relying only on the Future Land Use Map’s “commercial” designation for the property as “demonstrat[ing] that there
is a community need.” The developer in Tollbrook, LLC v. City of Troy, 2025 Mich. App. LEXIS 3711, No.368634 (May
13, 2025), also relied on the City of Troy’s Future Land Use map as the basis for its expectation that rezoning would follow
when it submitted an application to rezone single family residential property to a broader zoning district. The Future Land
Use Map there had also designated the developer’s parcels for future inclusion in the broader zoning district. In ruling
against the developer, the Michigan Court of Appeals stated that “it is understandable that the master plan’s designation,
combined with the properties’ proximity to a commercial district and major highway, may have contributed to that
expectation. But the structure of Michigan planning and zoning law makes clear that any such expectation would not be a
certainty. … Plaintiffs’ expectation that rezoning would follow as a matter of course lacks legal support.” Id. at 7-8. The

Opinion

PER CURIAM.

In this case, plaintiffs claim that the land use regulations imposed by defendant City of Troy burden their property to
such a degree that they constitute a regulatory taking under Const 1963, art 10, § 2. The trial court rejected that
claim, and we affirm.
I. FACTS

Safet Stafa owns the plaintiff businesses Tollbrook, LLC and Tollbrook West, LLC. Each business owns real
property in the City of Troy. Though near Big Beaver Road, the parcels do not front it and have been zoned "R-1B"
(single-family residential) since the 1960s. Plaintiffs seek to rezone their properties to "BB"—a reference to its
name, the "Big Beaver" district—so they may pursue a broader array of uses. The City of Troy's master plan,

                                                                                                            Page 143 of 261

--- packet page 144 ---

adopted under the Michigan Planning Enabling Act, MCL 125.3801 et seq., designated plaintiffs' [*2] properties for
                                     1
future inclusion in the BB district.

Although the course of dealings between plaintiffs and the City of Troy extends further back, the relevant timeline
begins in February 2019, when plaintiffs submitted "straight" (non-conditional) rezoning applications. Consistent
with the master plan, they asked to be placed in the BB district. The City of Troy's ordinance requires that "[t]he
proposed use of the property shall be indicated on the application," Troy Code, ch 39, § 16.03(B)(7), but both
applications stated that no specific development was planned at that time and that market research would
determine what uses were "feasible." Plaintiffs attached preliminary sketches of three-story mixed-use and
apartment buildings, but because these were not conditional rezoning requests under MCL 125.3405(1), the
illustrations did not bind the applicants to a particular use.

In May 2019, the City Planning Commission voted to recommend denial of the rezoning requests. At a City Council
meeting in July, nine individuals spoke in opposition to the rezoning during the public comment period. The Council
then voted 7-0 to adopt a resolution denying the rezoning requests, citing the absence of a proposed use and
                                                                                2
potential incompatibility [*3] with neighboring single-family residential lots.

Following that denial, plaintiffs filed separate lawsuits asserting two claims: denial of substantive due process and a
regulatory taking. The City of Troy removed the cases to federal court, but they were promptly remanded after the
court concluded that plaintiffs raised only state constitutional claims. On remand, the trial court granted summary
                                                                                                   3
disposition to the City of Troy under MCR 2.116(C)(8) on the substantive due process claims but denied it on the
regulatory takings claims and allowed discovery to proceed. After discovery, the City of Troy again moved for
summary disposition—this time under MCR 2.116(C)(10)—and the trial court granted it. Plaintiffs now appeal.
II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

We review de novo a trial court's decision on a motion for summary disposition. Maiden v Rozwood, 461 Mich 109,
118; 597 NW2d 817 (1999). In evaluating a motion under MCR 2.116(C)(10), courts "consider[] affidavits,
pleadings, depositions, admissions, and documentary evidence filed in the action or submitted by the parties, MCR
2.116(G)(5), in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion," and may grant summary disposition if that
evidence "show[s] that there is no genuine issue in respect to any material fact, and the moving party is entitled to
judgment [*4] as a matter of law." Quinto v Cross & Peters Co, 451 Mich 358, 362; 547 NW2d 314 (1996). The rule
uses a burden-shifting system:

       In presenting a motion for summary disposition, the moving party has the initial burden of supporting its
       position by affidavits, depositions, admissions, or other documentary evidence. The burden then shifts to the
       opposing party to establish that a genuine issue of disputed fact exists. Where the burden of proof at trial on a
       dispositive issue rests on a nonmoving party, the nonmoving party may not rely on mere allegations or denials
       in pleadings, but must go beyond the pleadings to set forth specific facts showing that a genuine issue of
       material fact exists. If the opposing party fails to present documentary evidence establishing the existence of a
       material factual dispute, the motion is properly granted. [Id. at 362-363 (citations omitted).]

To the extent this case involves the interpretation of statutes or ordinances, those issues are reviewed de novo.
Ford Motor Co v Woodhaven, 475 Mich 425, 438; 716 NW2d 247 (2006).

III. ANALYSIS

The trial court held that there was no genuine issue of material fact that the City of Troy's land use regulations did
not effect a taking of plaintiffs' property. We agree.

In K & K Constr, Inc v Dep't of Natural Resources, 456 Mich 570, 576-577; 575 NW2d 531 (1998), our Supreme
Court employed the federal framework for evaluating regulatory takings under the [*5] Michigan Constitution.
Likewise, the parties here agree that the appropriate standard is the balancing test articulated in Penn Central
Transp Co v City of New York, 438 U.S. 104; 98 S Ct 2646; 57 L Ed 2d 631 (1978). That test requires courts to
"engage in an 'ad hoc, factual inquiry,' centering on three factors: (1) the character of the government's action, (2)
the economic effect of the regulation on the property, and (3) the extent by which the regulation has interfered with
distinct, investment-backed expectations." K & K Constr, 456 Mich at 577, citing Penn Central, 438 U.S. at 124
(cleaned up).

At the outset, plaintiffs face a critical deficiency on the second factor, economic impact. In their briefing, plaintiffs
merely assert that "the City cannot seriously dispute" that rezoning would make their property "much more valuable"
than limiting it to single-family housing. That may be true in the abstract: a property would not become less valuable
by allowing its owners to use it in more ways. But the Penn Central inquiry requires more than logical
assumptions—it requires evidence:

    A property owner must provide some evidence of economic injury in order to succeed on the merits of a
    regulatory takings claim under the Penn Central test. The courts often decline to find any regulatory taking if
    the property owner continues using the property [*6] for some economic use despite the challenged
    restrictions. . . . The denial of a discretionary land use approval may effect an unconstitutional taking if it
    renders the property essentially unusable or economically valueless. If reasonable uses of the property still
    exist without the variance, however, the courts will reject a regulatory takings claim. [2 Salkin, American Law of
    Zoning (5th ed), § 16:13 (footnotes omitted).]

Indeed, Penn Central itself expressly referenced a case—with apparent approval—in which an 87.5% diminution of
value was not deemed a taking. See Penn Central, 438 U.S. at 131, citing Hadacheck v Sebastian, 239 U.S. 394;
36 S Ct 143; 60 L Ed 348 (1915). Here, plaintiffs offer no evidence—no appraisal report, no market analysis, no
expert affidavit—that would allow a court to weigh the extent of the economic impact. Lacking any such evidence,
this factor weighs heavily against plaintiffs.

As to the first factor—the character of the government's action—plaintiffs point to no physical occupation or forced
dedication of land. Instead, the City of Troy has simply declined to rezone property that has been zoned for single-
family residential use since the 1960s. "[T]he courts are unlikely to find any regulatory taking when the action can
be characterized as a garden-variety zoning [*7] law or another sort of traditional land use regulation." Salkin, §
16:15. As the Supreme Court explained: "A 'taking' may more readily be found when the interference with property
can be characterized as a physical invasion by government than when interference arises from some public
program adjusting the benefits and burdens of economic life to promote the common good." Penn Central, 438 U.S.
at 124 (citation omitted). At bottom, regulatory takings cases aim "to identify regulatory actions that are functionally
equivalent to the classic taking in which the government directly appropriates private property or ousts the owner
from his domain." Lingle v Chevron U.S.A., Inc, 544 U.S. 528, 539; 125 S Ct 2074; 161 L Ed 2d 876 (2005). This
case does not fall within that category, and plaintiffs cite no cases holding that land zoned single-family residential
constitutes a regulatory taking.

Finally, the third factor—the extent of interference with distinct, investment-backed expectations—at best offers
plaintiffs only limited support. Plaintiffs rely on the City of Troy's Future Land Use map, which designated their
parcels for future inclusion in the BB district, as the basis for their expectation that rezoning would follow. And it is
understandable that the master plan's designation, combined with the [*8] properties' proximity to a commercial
district and major highway, may have contributed to that expectation. But the structure of Michigan planning and
zoning law makes clear that any such expectation would not be a certainty. Zoning ordinances are enacted under
the Michigan Zoning Enabling Act, MCL 125.3101 et seq., while master plans are adopted under the Michigan

                                                                                                          Page 3 of 4
                                                                                                             Page 145 of 261

Planning Enabling Act, MCL 125.3801 et seq. The statutes set out distinct processes for taking action under them,
                                                                                   4
and while "[a] zoning ordinance shall be based upon a plan," MCL 125.3203(1), there is no indication in either
statute that changes to a master plan mandate corresponding changes to the zoning ordinance. Indeed, they
cannot: a zoning ordinance must be adopted by a municipality's legislative body, MCL 125.3401(5), while a master
                                                                               5
plan can be approved by a planning commission alone, MCL 125.3843(2). Plaintiffs' expectation that rezoning
would follow as a matter of course lacks legal support. Moreover, they have not offered, for instance, testimony
                                                                                                                 6
from another developer with experience in Troy indicating that such an expectation would have been reasonable.
As a result, this factor at most slightly favors plaintiffs—but not enough to overcome the weight of the other two
         7
factors.

In sum, plaintiffs offered no evidence of the economic impact of the challenged regulation; the character of the
government's action weighs against a taking; and while the extent of interference with investment-backed
expectations may modestly support plaintiffs' position, it does not meaningfully tip the weight of an otherwise one-
sided scale. Because the Penn Central factors do not support plaintiffs' claim, there is no genuine issue of material
fact as to whether the City of Troy effected a taking. It did not. We therefore affirm the trial court's decision.

Cumulative Environmental and Public Cost Considerations

The northwest marsh of Asylum Lake is among the most ecologically sensitive areas of the preserve. It
supports wetland-dependent bird species, including waterfowl that rely on quiet shoreline habitat for
nesting, as well as turtle species identified as being of Special Concern status in Michigan. These species

intensive commercial development.

Asylum Lake has already required costly environmental remediation to address impacts from road runoff.
Further degradation of the system’s natural filtration capacity risks shifting future mitigation and
infrastructure costs onto the public. From a long-term planning and fiscal perspective, preserving
functioning natural systems is more responsible than relying on engineered solutions after damage has
occurred.

Land-Use Longevity and Irreversibility

Commercial retail developments such as strip malls typically have a relatively short functional lifespan.
Shifts in retail patterns, tenant turnover, and market demand often render such sites obsolete within a few
decades, after which they are frequently underutilized, redeveloped, or abandoned. Lakes and wetland
systems, by contrast, function on ecological timescales measured in centuries when protected, but once
degraded are extraordinarily difficult—and often impossible—to restore to their original condition.

Rezoning land immediately adjacent to Asylum Lake for intensive commercial use effectively trades a
permanent public asset for a land use with a limited and uncertain lifespan, while locking in long-term
environmental impacts and public mitigation costs that persist long after private economic benefits have
faded.

                     Conclusion (Analysis of PC Staff Report):
The Staff Report’s favorable recommendation is unsound because it is not grounded in a complete or
accurate application of the City’s adopted plans and rezoning criteria. Across multiple criteria, the
analysis relies on generalized corridor characterizations, treats the Future Land Use Map as
determinative, and defers key unresolved questions to future regulatory processes. It omits clear
language in adopted neighborhood plans, relevant site history, documented public opposition, and site-
specific environmental constraints that are both substantial in scale and environmentally sensitive,
warranting heightened scrutiny under adopted policy.

shifted from the Applicant to the City, contrary to the requirements of the Zoning Ordinance.[25]

As a result, the Staff Report neither presents a complete, policy-grounded case for rezoning
nor applies the burden of proof as required under Sec. 9.3, and therefore should not support
approval of the requested amendment.

Conclusion (Analysis of Applicant Rezoning Application):
The Applicant’s Rezoning Application does not satisfy the requirements for a rezoning under the City’s
Zoning Ordinance. Across all five criteria, the Applicant relies on generalized assertions, repeated
references to the Future Land Use Map, and corridor-level characterizations, rather than providing site-
specific evidence or policy support. The application fails to meaningfully engage the City’s Strategic
Vision, omits controlling guidance in the ODWN Plan, and does not demonstrate changed conditions,
community need, compatibility with sensitive adjacent uses, or a logical and orderly development pattern
at the parcel level. Accordingly, the Applicant has not met the burden of proof required under Sec.
9.3, and the application does not establish a sufficient basis for approval of the requested rezoning.

Dear Commissioners and City Staff,

My name is Jenny Doezema, Executive Director of the Kalamazoo Climate Crisis Coalition. 340 East
Michigan. I am writing this letter in strong opposition to the request to rezone 4301 Stadium Drive
from Residential to Commercial, and respectfully ask that you please deny the zoning change request.

A zoning change to Commercial could permanently harm the environment of the adjacent Asylum
Lake Preserve, further threatens the climate adaptation potential of Kalamazoo County, and does not
meet the Planning Commission’s 5 Conditions for Rezoning:

The zoning change is not consistent with Imagine Kalamazoo’s 2035 Strategic Vision Goals Topic
   #4, Environment, which states “A healthy environment sustains community well-being today and
   for generations to come.” It continues with a goal to “preserve, restore, and mitigate damage to the
   natural areas including preserves, creeks, and rivers.

   The Imagine Kalamazoo 2025 Master Plan prioritized Environmental Responsibility, climate
      resilience, and neighborhood compatibility. Rezoning this parcel for commercial use is a direct
      threat to the health of Asylum Lake threatening already compromised water quality and
      ecosystems the lake and watershed supports. Furthermore, in a time deeply threatened by the
      climate crisis any threat or removal of natural systems, still intact, which support adaptation
      and resilience should be denied. The time to make choices to protect our future and that of our
      grandchildren is now.

   Any decision to remove forested land should be carefully scrutinized as this would decrease local
     capacity to sequester CO2, decrease air quality improvement potential, and decrease habitat
     which ultimately supports pollination for our local food system, and decreases local potential
     for a clean water supply.

   The City's Public Participation Policy (2022) requires meaningful engagement and a summary of
      how community input changed proposals. Thousands of residents have asked the City to
      expand and protect natural areas. What’s in the record doesn’t meet that standard. When a site
      is this sensitive, process matters. The public shouldn’t be treated as an afterthought.

Are there conditions that have changed that support the zoning change?

    • No, in fact, as climate change continues to occur, it is increasingly imperative that green spaces
    be preserved. Commercial development next to Asylum Lake would create an urban heat hotspot
             and loss of CO2 sequestration by replacing vegetation with impervious surfaces.

   ●​   Ecological Connectivity: The wildlife that inhabits Westnedge Hill and Kleinstuck Preserve, including
        many migratory bird species—relies on a network of healthy green spaces across the city. Encroaching
        on Asylum Lake with commercial development, increased light pollution, and impervious surfaces will
        disrupt the ecological corridor that makes Kalamazoo a unique place to live.
   ●​   Contradicting the Master Plan: The Imagine Kalamazoo 2035 Strategic Vision promised a focus on
        environmental responsibility and the protection of our natural assets. Forcing commercial development
        onto a site that currently serves as a vital ecological buffer is a direct violation of the goals the
        community worked so hard to establish.
   ●​   Traffic and Over-Saturation: As community members who frequently travel the Stadium Drive corridor to
        access west-side services, we can attest that additional commercial entrances and increased traffic at
        this specific location are unnecessary. The existing commercial nodes at Drake and Stadium are
        sufficient. We do not need to sacrifice a preserve buffer for more retail space.
Protecting the Asylum Lake Preserve is a city-wide priority, not just a localized concern. We ask the
Commission to honor the Master Plan and protect the integrity of our natural areas by denying the rezoning
request for 4301 Stadium Drive.
