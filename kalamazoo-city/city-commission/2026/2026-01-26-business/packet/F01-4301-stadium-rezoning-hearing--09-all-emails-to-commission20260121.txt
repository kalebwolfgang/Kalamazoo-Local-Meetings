Dear City Commissioners and Planning Department,

My name is Erin Taylor, and I am a 25-year-old resident living directly off the Asylum Lake
Preserve. I’m writing to respectfully voice my opposition to the request to rezone 4301 Stadium
Drive to CC Commercial.

This 18.9-acre parcel is environmentally sensitive and historically significant, sitting less than 250
feet from Asylum Lake and only about 35 feet from its wetlands. It was once part of a colonial-era
farmstead dating back to around 1840, one of Kalamazoo’s oldest homesteads. The historic home was
demolished in 2022, but the property still contains remnants of the “Fruitbelt Line” railway, which is
now being restored nearby as a regionally significant rail-to-trail.

Allowing commercial rezoning here would permanently erase both cultural heritage and ecological
integrity.

From an environmental standpoint, construction and commercial activity would disturb fragile soils,
alter groundwater flow, and increase pollution from road salts, oils, and runoff. Fifty years of studies
have shown that commercial development degrades Asylum Lake’s water quality, threatening the
ecosystem the community has fought for decades to preserve.

This rezoning proposal also fails the City’s own planning standards:
  • It is inconsistent with the Imagine Kalamazoo 2035 Strategic Vision, which prioritizes
preserving natural spaces and sustainability.
  • There are no changed conditions justifying the request—the community continues to oppose
commercial zoning here.
  • It does not address a community need, given that more than 70 commercial properties are
already for sale within city limits.
  • It is not compatible with surrounding residential and protected natural zoning.
  • And it would not promote logical or orderly development, especially at one of Kalamazoo’s most
dangerous intersections, Stadium and Drake.

Asylum Lake is one of the few remaining places in Kalamazoo that offers quiet, reflection, and clean air—
values that align with the city’s stated goals for a healthy and sustainable future. Once this land is
developed, we cannot bring it back.

What I’m focusing on is salinization — the addition of salt — in freshwater areas of the Kalamazoo River Watershed.
Not all lakes are as privileged as Asylum Lake, which has multiple studies by McLeod, Sibert, and Koretsky that
chemically analyze Asylum Lake as an urban freshwater lake affected particularly by salt pollution. Salt in our
environment comes primarily from road salt — that’s the stuff that keeps drivers safe in the winter, so we don’t want to
eliminate it entirely; however, the very salt that protects us is getting into our water systems, our soil, and the very
groundwater we drink. Let me put it into economic terms: salty Asylum Lake means salty Kalamazoo water, which
causes Kalamazoo drinking water, pipes, crops, and lakes to suffer big time. I know many people are worried about
Asylum Lake as a nature preserve, but I also know that commercial development has absolutely zero regard for that,
and I want to make sure the city knows that the research going on at Asylum Lake is in the city’s best interest — it helps
keep Kalamazoo water clean.

Road salt is affecting Asylum Lake — the lake doesn’t go through its regular water cycling every year anymore

The top predictor of elevated chloride, aka measurable environmental traces of salt, in a watershed, according

agriculture combined What’s an impervious surface, you might ask? Literally anything that is built on land that
doesn’t allow water to infiltrate the ground as it should Why is that a predictor of saltiness, you might ask?

Re: Rezoning Request — 4301 Stadium Drive
Dear Mayor, Vice-Mayor, and Members of the City Commission,
The Stewards of Kleinstuck respectfully submit this letter in opposition to the proposed rezoning of the parcel now
identified as 4301 Stadium Drive, located immediately adjacent to the northwestern boundary of the Asylum Lake
Preserve. We support the Planning Commission’s unanimous decision to deny the proposed rezoning.
Community Stewardship and Demonstrated Capacity
We write not only as advocates for environmental protection, but as an organization with a demonstrated history of
stewardship, collaboration, and financial investment in Kalamazoo’s natural assets. In 2021, our community successfully
raised the funds necessary to purchase and permanently protect the 12-acre parcel now known as Little Sister Preserve,
adjacent to the Kleinstuck Preserve. That effort reflects a constituency that is willing and able to partner with the City to
safeguard land when meaningful opportunities arise.

Public concern regarding the rezoning of 4301 Stadium Drive should be understood in this context. It reflects sustained
engagement, long-term commitment, and a clear expectation that adopted plans and protections will be applied
consistently, particularly in areas directly adjacent to irreplaceable public natural resources.
Consistency with the City of Kalamazoo Master Plan
The Kalamazoo 2025 Master Plan emphasizes Environmental Responsibility, including the mitigation of stormwater
runoff, erosion, and contamination of water resources. The parcel at 4301 Stadium Drive is hydrologically connected to
the northwest marsh of Asylum Lake, a wetland system that serves as a primary retention and filtration area for a large
upstream drainage basin to the north and west.
Rezoning this parcel to the City’s most intensive commercial classification would introduce additional impervious surface
and land disturbance that is incompatible with these Master Plan objectives. Increased runoff volume and pollutant load
would be directed toward a system already under strain from surrounding roadway infrastructure, undermining the City’s
stated environmental goals.
Cumulative Environmental and Public Cost Considerations
The northwest marsh of Asylum Lake is among the most ecologically sensitive areas of the preserve. It supports wetland-
dependent bird species, including waterfowl that rely on quiet shoreline habitat for nesting, as well as turtle species
identified as being of Special Concern status in Michigan. These species are particularly vulnerable to increased noise,
light, runoff, and habitat fragmentation associated with intensive commercial development.
Asylum Lake has already required costly environmental remediation to address impacts from road runoff. Further
degradation of the system’s natural filtration capacity risks shifting future mitigation and infrastructure costs onto the
public. From a long-term planning and fiscal perspective, preserving functioning natural systems is more responsible than
relying on engineered solutions after damage has occurred.
Land-Use Longevity and Irreversibility
Commercial retail developments such as strip malls typically have a relatively short functional lifespan. Shifts in retail
patterns, tenant turnover, and market demand often render such sites obsolete within a few decades, after which they are
frequently underutilized, redeveloped, or abandoned. Lakes and wetland systems, by contrast, function on ecological
timescales measured in centuries when protected, but once degraded are extraordinarily difficult—and often impossible—
to restore to their original condition.
Rezoning land immediately adjacent to Asylum Lake for intensive commercial use effectively trades a permanent public
asset for a land use with a limited and uncertain lifespan, while locking in long-term environmental impacts and public
mitigation costs that persist long after private economic benefits have faded.
Conclusion
Rezoning the parcel at 4301 Stadium Drive to the most intensive commercial classification is inconsistent with the City’s
Master Plan and contrary to long-term environmental and fiscal stewardship. We respectfully urge the City Commission to
deny the rezoning request and to uphold the policies and protections that safeguard Kalamazoo’s natural resources for
current and future generations.
Thank you for your thoughtful consideration.

The land at 4301 Stadium Drive drops quickly to the wetland on the north side of Asylum Lake – at its most
precipitous, the bank plunges 30 feet down to the water’s edge. Currently, trees and other vegetation stabilize
those steep banks and keep soil from contaminating the wetland below. Even with the Natural Features
Protection buffering the water, development of the site that removes this protective layer of anchoring
vegetation will expose these banks to erosion, resulting in considerable soil sediment entering the waterway.
Sediment pollution threatens aquatic plant and animal life through smothering, reduced oxygen levels, and
blocking light that’s necessary for plant photosynthesis. Precedent for this was set in 2019 when nearby
construction caused a large amount of sediment to enter Asylum Lake, resulting in widespread media
coverage when Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes & Energy cited Western Michigan
University with unlawful discharge.

The quality of Asylum Lake’s water is intrinsically linked to the health and use of the land that surrounds it.
Nestled in a bowl between two busy throughfares, it is especially vulnerable to pollution from chemical and
sediment impervious surface runoff – which often includes road salt, microplastics and other tiny particles
from tire wear, fuel hydrocarbons, and heavy metals such as lead. A 2008 study, cited in the 2023 Asylum Lake
Management Plan, noted high levels of chemical pollutants, including mercury, entering the lake via storm
drains near Drake Road and Stadium Drive. Since the time of that study, this active shopping area has seen
significant development that has likely increased the chemical load reaching Asylum Lake. Commercial
development at 4301 Stadium Drive would compound this effect even more, perhaps very significantly
depending on the type of business in operation. The increased water contamination could cause unhealthy
algae blooms, harm aquatic plants and animals, and pollute water that will ultimately reach the Kalamazoo
River and Lake Michigan. One might dismiss the damage caused by pollution at Asylum Lake as insignificant
compared to the many inputs it receives in its long journey to Lake Michigan, but water quality suffers from a
thousand cuts, and most are preventable.

The Asylum Lake area is an incredible complex from a conservation lens and offers homes, places to nest,
food, and the ability to find mates without crossing high-traffic roads to many kinds of animals, including
eagles, hawks, waterfowl, songbirds, fish, beaver, frogs, and turtles. But it’s a benefit to humans, too. Walking
and watching wildlife are inexpensive pleasures shared by many. The lakes and surrounding preserve provide
a much-needed urban oasis for people within the city, in a world that has fewer natural places to go by the
day. Asylum Lake Preserve provides a temporary escape from urban noise and busy-ness, something that
would be compromised by the development of the northwest corner. Many studies now prove that increased
access to greenspace (that which is easily reached by bike, car, or bus) provides a suite of community benefits
that include improvements to both mental and physical health – especially for children.

SWMLC works across the 3.2 million acres of southwest Michigan, and we can tell you that having a 274-acre
natural area within the city limits is almost unheard of. While many other communities have city parks and
small natural spaces, the presence of such a large natural area in Kalamazoo sets it apart from any other
community we work in. It is undoubtedly one of the reasons people choose to live, work, and play in this city
and contribute to its economic base through tax dollars and spending at local businesses. It is also powerfully
attractive to potential new residents who include greenspace in their list of must-haves.

development. We believe that, along with SWMLC’s Bow in the Clouds Nature Preserve and WMU’s Kleinstuck
